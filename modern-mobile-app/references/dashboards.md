# Dashboard Patterns (reference)

Source: Mobbin, "I Studied 2,108 Dashboards To See What Sticks" (2,108 dashboard screens across 536 apps & websites).
Core thesis: **most dashboards show you the data; the best ones almost protect you from it.** The word "dashboard" once meant the board shielding carriage passengers from flying mud — its job is still to put a clean surface between the user and the mess. The difference is not spacing, grids, or polish: it's whether the designer decides what the user needs to see, interprets it, anticipates the moment, and restrains everything else.

## Patterns that work

- **The first screen makes the pitch.** ~1 in 5 apps put the dashboard on the home screen, and in about four seconds the product has made its case or not. Shopify's Live View shows a globe with real-time visitor dots — encoding a feeling, not just a session count; Front compresses a week of support volume into a three-second heatmap; Navattic names the actual companies watching your demo ("Dropbox: 8 visitors, 8m 16s") — the dashboard is the proof of purchase.
- **Read the data for the user.** Fey annotates a portfolio chart with key events and earnings dates, surfacing the "why" behind a drop before anxiety hits. Runna packages a finished run into a sequenced recap (detail, effort rating, milestone celebration, lap breakdown) — celebrating you before you've decided how to feel. Origin puts a daily market brief beside your portfolio chart so you don't spiral into a news site.
- **Coin your metrics.** Duolingo turned "consecutive days" into a "streak" — a word implying momentum and risk worth protecting. Oura collapses HRV, sleep stages, and respiratory rate into one named Readiness Score you check before getting out of bed. Apple Watch's "rings" became everyday vocabulary even among non-owners.
- **Answer the "so what" before they ask.** Amplitude's Anomaly Detection leads with one sentence ("308 anomalies flagged since June 1") that contextualizes the whole chart. Linktree names the spike and explains the cause before you've looked at the chart. Binance puts an AI summary of the Fear & Greed chart above the data: verdict first, evidence second.
- **Know what to leave out.** Plausible shows five stats, one chart, two columns — blank space where competitors put hundreds of reports, detail one click away. Revolut, despite exchange rates, crypto, vaults, and investing, makes the home screen just your balance plus three buttons.

## Rules of thumb

- "Does your first screen capture what your product is?"
- "Anticipate the emotional state. Then design for it." (Ask: how do I want them to feel when they open this?)
- "Give users vocabulary for accomplishments. Leaving your product feels like losing them."
- "Answer the 'so what' before they have to ask." Verdict first, evidence second.
- "Blank space is part of the design." Detail exists one click/tap away, not on the surface.
- Minimalism isn't the goal; surfacing the one thing that matters is.

## Warnings

- More charts, metrics, tabs, and analysis is the default failure mode — you end up with a "summarized mess" on top of the original mess.
- Don't show raw numbers when the user needs an explanation; don't explain when they need raw data — you must make the executive decision.
- Don't fill empty space just because you can: AI can generate a dashboard in seconds — the hard part of design is knowing what to leave out.
